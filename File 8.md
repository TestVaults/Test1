Upon the eleventh day of solitude, when no living soul had crossed her sight, the keeper of the lighthouse spied a boat upon the horizon—small it was, and stubborn 'gainst the grey and heaving swell. She turned her ancient glass of brass upon it and did count but one poor soul aboard, who rowed with the uneven, halting stroke of one ne'er schooled in such an art, yet would not, for all that, cease his labor. The tempest was not looked for till the fall of night, granting the rower some four hours' grace to win the rocks—rocks that, e'en so, might prove unkind to him. Yet she did kindle the signal fire all the same, not for that the lighthouse had need of it, but for that eleven days of silence had taught her this: some laws are made but to be broken for a stranger worth the breaking.

Mireille did inherit the bakery upon a Tuesday, and by Thursday next the debt collectors had found her out. Her grandmother, ere she passed, had left instructions taped within the flour bin: let the dough proof through the night, ne'er hasten the caramel, and place no trust in any man who praises the bread before he hath tasted it. Yet none of these commandments spake to the matter of the men in grey doublets who came asking after a walk-in frost-chamber that, so far as Mireille did know, had ne'er held aught but loaves of bread.

In the month of March she found a note, wedged behind a loosened brick where oven met the wall—penned in her grandmother's looping hand, and dated some two-score years before, bearing coordinates in place of ingredients. Mireille had grown believing the bakery's only secret was a pinch of cardamom stirred into the morning buns. Long she sat upon the flour-dusted floor, the note a-tremble in her hand, and did reckon anew all she thought she knew of a woman who was wont to hum whilst kneading dough at break of day.

The coordinates led, most strange, to a place scarce two streets hence, beneath the fountain in the town square where children through the generations had cast their coins. Mireille did tell herself she would not go a-seeking, that some inheritances are better left as flour and butter and the perfume of rising yeast in a warm chamber. Yet after the closing of her shop she found herself there still, apron and all, watching the coins gleam 'neath the streetlamp's light, as things that ask to be understood.

She did not dig. Not that night. Instead she went home and did make a new manner of bread, one that kept her grandmother's own receipt but folded in the zest of orange and a whisper of anise—somewhat wholly her own, stitched upon somewhat inherited. When her customers did ask what had changed, she only smiled and said she was at last attending to what had been there all along.

By such time as the men in grey doublets ceased their calling, Mireille had ceased to need the knowledge of what lay buried 'neath the fountain. Some mornings still she touched the loosened brick behind the oven, feeling for the edge of the note, a reminder that folk do carry more history than ever their bread lets show. She kept on baking. She kept on humming, ill enough, as her grandmother once did, and found that some inheritances are best spent slow, one warm loaf at a time.
